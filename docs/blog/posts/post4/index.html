The question

In June 2016 the UK voted to leave the European Union. It formally left in January 2020 and exited the single market and customs union at the end of that year. Ten years after the vote, economists still disagree about what it cost. The difficulty is that we never observe the UK that stayed.

This post takes a simple approach to that problem. I compare the UK with the other six G7 economies, before and after the referendum, on three measures where Brexit should show up if it mattered: output, investment, and trade. If the UK tracked its peers before 2016 and fell behind afterwards, that is evidence consistent with a Brexit cost. If it did not, the simple story needs rethinking.

Data and method

The data come from the World Bank’s World Development Indicators, downloaded with the WDI package in R for the seven G7 countries from 2000 to 2025. I use three indicators:

  • Real GDP per capita (NY.GDP.PCAP.KD, constant 2015 US dollars)
  • Gross fixed capital formation as a share of GDP (NE.GDI.FTOT.ZS)
  • Trade, exports plus imports, as a share of GDP (NE.TRD.GNFS.ZS)

Raw levels are not comparable across these countries, so each series is transformed before plotting.

  • GDP is measured per capita and in real terms, then indexed to 2016 = 100. Per capita matters because population growth differs across the G7, and indexing puts every country on the same scale in the referendum year.
  • Investment and trade shares are shown as the change in percentage points since 2016. Germany trades a far larger share of its GDP than the United States does, so comparing levels would mostly show country size. Comparing changes shows who moved.

The comparison group is the unweighted average of Canada, France, Germany, Italy, Japan, and the United States. I keep only years in which all seven countries report data, so the average never changes composition. This is why investment and trade end in 2024.

This is a descriptive comparison and not a causal estimate. The G7 average stands in for what might have happened to the UK without Brexit, and the limitations section discusses how well it does that job.

1. Output: a gap that opened after exit, not after the vote

The referendum itself left no visible mark on output. In 2019, three years after the vote, the UK index stood at 104.3 against 104.1 for its peers.

The gap appears in 2020. The UK’s pandemic recession was deeper, leaving it 4.8 points behind. It recovered part of that ground by 2022, when the gap narrowed to 1.4 points, and then fell back again. In 2025 real GDP per capita was 5.5% above its 2016 level in the UK and 9.0% above in the peer group, a shortfall of 3.4 points. Among the seven countries, the UK ranks 5 out of 7 for growth since 2016.

Is a gap of that size unusual? Not by itself. Because every country is set to 100 in 2016, the chart also shows relative growth before the referendum: between 2011 and 2016 the UK outgrew its peers by 3.2 points. Swings of this size have happened before, in the UK’s favour. What stands out now is the direction and the persistence: the shortfall was 3.1 points in 2023, 3.6 in 2024, and 3.4 in 2025, well after pandemic restrictions ended.

2. Investment: a recovery that stalled

Investment is where economists expected the referendum to bite first, because firms facing uncertainty about future market access tend to postpone spending. The data fit that story, with a caveat.

By 2024 the peer group’s investment share was 1.4 percentage points above its 2016 level. The UK’s was 0.1 points above, essentially unchanged. A gap was already visible in 2019 (0.6 points) and has stayed between 1 and 2 points since 2020.

The caveat is that the two lines were far from parallel before 2016. UK investment had been climbing out of a deep post-crisis trough: its share of GDP rose 2.4 points between 2011 and 2016 while the peer average was flat. The honest reading is that UK investment did not fall after the referendum. A recovery stopped, at the moment when investment in comparable economies began to rise. Note also that this indicator covers all fixed investment, including housing and government, and not business investment alone.

3. Trade: the weakest evidence

Trade should give the cleanest test, since leaving the single market raised trade barriers on a known date. The timing does line up. Through 2019 the UK’s trade share had risen faster than its peers’, by 2.7 points, plausibly because the fall in sterling after the referendum raised the pound value of both imports and exports. In 2021, the first year outside the single market, the gap flipped to 3.1 points behind, and it was 1.8 points behind in 2024.

But the UK series is volatile, and that undermines the result. In 2015, before any of this, the UK was already 4.2 points below the peer average on the same measure. The post-2021 gap sits inside the range of gaps seen before the referendum. The direction is what a Brexit effect would predict, but on annual trade shares I cannot distinguish it from ordinary noise. The spike in 2022 across both lines likely reflects energy prices, which raise the nominal value of imports everywhere.

Explore the data

The chart below combines the three measures. Use the buttons to switch between them, hover over any year to see the size of the gap, and drag the slider at the bottom to zoom in on a period.

Code 
col_uk   <- "#C8102E"
col_peer <- "#1F3A5F"

measures <- c(
  gdp_pc_idx = "Real GDP per capita (2016 = 100)",
  invest_chg = "Investment share of GDP (pp change since 2016)",
  trade_chg  = "Trade share of GDP (pp change since 2016)"
)
button_labels <- c("GDP per capita", "Investment", "Trade")
events <- data.frame(year  = c(2016, 2021),
                     label = c("Referendum", "Leaves single market"))

p <- plot_ly()
for (i in seq_along(measures)) {
  d <- filter(gap, measure == names(measures)[i])
  p <- p |>
    add_trace(
      x = d$year, y = d$peer, type = "scatter", mode = "lines",
      name = "G7 average (ex-UK)", visible = (i == 1),
      line = list(color = col_peer, dash = "dash", width = 2),
      hovertemplate = "G7 avg: %{y:.1f}<extra></extra>"
    ) |>
    add_trace(
      x = d$year, y = d$uk, type = "scatter", mode = "lines+markers",
      name = "United Kingdom", visible = (i == 1),
      line = list(color = col_uk, width = 3),
      marker = list(color = col_uk, size = 5),
      fill = "tonexty", fillcolor = "rgba(200,16,46,0.12)",
      text = sprintf("%+.1f", d$gap),
      hovertemplate = "UK: %{y:.1f}<br>Gap vs peers: %{text}<extra></extra>"
    )
}

buttons <- lapply(seq_along(measures), function(i) {
  vis <- rep(FALSE, 2 * length(measures))
  vis[c(2 * i - 1, 2 * i)] <- TRUE
  list(method = "update", label = button_labels[i],
       args = list(list(visible = vis),
                   list("yaxis.title.text" = measures[[i]])))
})

vlines <- lapply(events$year, function(x) {
  list(type = "line", x0 = x, x1 = x, yref = "paper", y0 = 0, y1 = 1,
       line = list(color = "grey", dash = "dot", width = 1))
})

annots <- lapply(seq_len(nrow(events)), function(i) {
  list(x = events$year[i], y = c(1, 0.93)[i], yref = "paper",
       text = events$label[i], showarrow = FALSE,
       xanchor = "right", yanchor = "top",
       font = list(size = 11, color = "grey"))
})

p |>
  layout(
    hovermode = "x unified",

Limitations

  • Other shocks hit at the same time. The UK left the single market in the middle of a pandemic and one year before an energy crisis to which its gas-dependent economy was especially exposed. A comparison with peers cannot separate these from Brexit.
  • The G7 average is an imperfect counterfactual. The investment and trade charts show that the UK did not move in parallel with its peers before 2016, so some of the later divergence may have happened anyway. A synthetic control that weights countries to match the UK’s pre-2016 path would be the natural next step.
  • Annual data hide timing. Events such as the referendum in June and the end of the transition period in December fall inside single observations.
  • Trade shares are nominal. They move with exchange rates and commodity prices as well as with trade volumes.

Takeaway

Three charts give three different strengths of answer. Output gives the clearest signal: the UK kept pace with the G7 for three years after the referendum, then fell about 3 points behind after it actually left and has not recovered the ground. A swing of similar size, in the UK’s favour, occurred in the five years before the referendum, so this alone is not proof. Investment points the same way, as a recovery that stalled while peers’ investment rose. Trade does not settle anything at this level of aggregation.

The broader lesson is about timing. The damage many expected from the vote itself does not appear in output. What the data show is a slower divergence that began once the new trading arrangements took effect, which is also when it became hardest to tell apart from everything else that went wrong in 2020.
