One Theorem, Four Lemmas: The Envelope Theorem in Microeconomics

economics
microeconomics
Published

September 14, 2026

The question

Suppose the price of gasoline rises from $3.00 to $3.10 a gallon. How much worse off are you?

The honest answer seems to require a lot of work. You might drive a little less, combine errands, or take the bus once a week. To measure the damage properly you would have to work out how you re-optimise your whole budget at the new price, then compare.

The envelope theorem says you can skip all of that. For a small price change, the damage is simply

\[\text{loss} \approx (\text{gallons you currently buy}) \times (\text{price increase}).\]

If you buy 40 gallons a month, a 10-cent rise costs you about $4 a month, whatever you end up doing about it.

This matters for two reasons. First, it is how economists price small policy changes in practice. Estimating the cost of a new tax, or a firm’s exposure to a wage rise, rarely requires a model of how everyone will substitute; current quantities are enough. Second, intermediate microeconomics teaches four results as if they were separate facts: Shephard’s Lemma, Hotelling’s Lemma, Roy’s Identity and the Le Chatelier principle. Each has its own proof. This post argues that all four are the gasoline calculation above, applied to different problems, and that once you see this you only need to remember one idea.

The intuition: the top of a hill is flat

You have already chosen the best bundle at the current prices. That means you are at the top of a hill: every small change to your choices, such as one gallon more or less, leaves you no better off.

When the price moves, two things happen:

  1. Direct effect. The bundle you already hold becomes more expensive. This is the 40 gallons × 10 cents.
  2. Indirect effect. You re-optimise, shifting your choices to the new best bundle.

The indirect effect is a small movement away from the top of the hill. At the top, the ground is flat, so a small step changes your height by almost nothing. That is why the indirect effect can be ignored for small changes, and why only the direct effect is left. The rest of the post is this sentence written in different notation.

Checking it with numbers

Before the general proof, it is worth confirming that the shortcut actually works. Take a consumer with Cobb-Douglas utility \(u(x_1,x_2) = x_1^{0.5}x_2^{0.5}\) who needs to reach utility \(\bar u = 1\), with \(p_2 = 1\). At \(p_1 = 1\) she buys one unit of good 1. The code below raises \(p_1\) by different amounts and compares two numbers:

  • Exact: the true extra spending needed to stay equally well off, after she fully re-optimises.
  • Shortcut: current quantity × price change, ignoring any re-optimisation.
Show code 
a <- 0.5; u_bar <- 1; p2 <- 1; p0 <- 1

# Expenditure function for Cobb-Douglas (closed form)
e <- function(p1) u_bar * (p1 / a)^a * (p2 / (1 - a))^(1 - a)
x1_now <- a * e(p0) / p0          # quantity of good 1 bought at p0

dp <- c(0.01, 0.05, 0.10, 0.25, 0.50, 1.00)
check <- data.frame(
  rise     = dp,
  exact    = e(p0 + dp) - e(p0),
  shortcut = x1_now * dp
)
check$error_pct <- 100 * (check$shortcut - check$exact) / check$exact

knitr::kable(check, digits = c(2, 4, 4, 1),
             col.names = c("Price rise", "Exact cost increase",
                           "Shortcut (x1 × rise)", "Shortcut error (%)"))

For a 1% price rise the shortcut is off by about a quarter of a percent. For a 10% rise it is off by about 2%. Only when the price doubles does the error become large, about 20%. Two things stand out:

  • The shortcut always overstates the cost. It assumes she keeps buying the old bundle, when in fact she can substitute toward the cheaper good and soften the blow.
  • The dollar gap between the two grows with the square of the price change. Doubling the price rise roughly quadruples the gap (and doubles the percentage error). That is the “flat top of the hill” in numbers: the benefit of re-optimising is a second-order effect.

The same logic explains a well-known problem with official inflation measures. A fixed-basket price index like the CPI uses the shortcut: it prices last period’s basket at this period’s prices. As the table shows, that overstates how much more it costs to stay equally well off, and the bias is larger when prices move a lot. This is the “substitution bias” in the CPI.

The general result

Now the same argument in symbols. Let \(f(x,\theta)\) be what we are maximising (utility, profit), \(x\) the choice, and \(\theta\) a parameter we do not control (a price). Write \(x^*(\theta)\) for the best choice and \(V(\theta) = f(x^*(\theta),\theta)\) for the best achievable value. By the chain rule,

\[\frac{dV}{d\theta} = \underbrace{\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\bigg|_{x^*}\frac{dx^*}{d\theta}}_{\text{indirect: re-optimising}} \;+\; \underbrace{\frac{\partial f}{\partial \theta}\bigg|_{x^*}}_{\text{direct}}.\]

At the optimum the first-order condition says \(\partial f/\partial x = 0\), which is the flat hilltop. So the indirect term vanishes and

\[\boxed{\;\frac{dV}{d\theta} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial \theta}\bigg|_{x = x^*(\theta)}\;}\]

Notice what we never had to compute: \(dx^*/d\theta\), how the choice responds. That is often the hardest part of a problem, and the theorem lets us avoid it.

With a budget or other constraint. Most economic problems have a constraint, \(g(x,\theta) = 0\), and then \(\partial f/\partial x\) is not zero at the optimum, because you are pushed against the constraint rather than sitting on a free hilltop. The fix is to include the constraint in the objective with a Lagrange multiplier \(\lambda\), \(\mathcal{L} = f - \lambda g\). The Lagrangian is flat at the optimum in exactly the way \(f\) was before, so the same argument goes through:

\[\frac{dV}{d\theta} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \theta}\bigg|_{x^*,\,\lambda^*} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial \theta} - \lambda^* \frac{\partial g}{\partial \theta}.\]

In words: differentiate the Lagrangian with respect to the parameter and hold everything else fixed. The second term has a clean meaning. \(\lambda^*\) is the value of relaxing the constraint by one unit, so if the parameter also tightens the constraint, that cost is priced at \(\lambda^*\). The same result holds for minimisation problems, since minimising \(f\) is the same as maximising \(-f\).

Four lemmas, one idea

Each famous result below is one application of the boxed formula. In every case the recipe is the same: write down the problem, pick the price that changes, and ask, “If I hold my current choices fixed, how much does this price change cost or earn me?” The table summarises; the paragraphs explain what each result means.

Result Problem Price that changes Direct effect, holding choices fixed Conclusion
Shephard’s Lemma Minimise spending to reach utility \(\bar u\) Price of good \(i\) You pay extra on each unit you buy \(\partial e/\partial p_i = h_i\)
Hotelling’s Lemma Maximise profit Output price \(p\) You earn extra on each unit you sell \(\partial \pi/\partial p = y^*\)
Roy’s Identity Maximise utility with income \(m\) Price of good \(i\) Like losing income equal to \(x_i\) \(x_i^* = -\dfrac{\partial v/\partial p_i}{\partial v/\partial m}\)
Le Chatelier Minimise cost, short run vs long run Input price \(w_1\) Same, but one problem has fewer choices Long-run demand responds more

Shephard’s Lemma is the gasoline example. The expenditure function \(e(p,\bar u)\) is the least you can spend to reach utility \(\bar u\). Raise \(p_i\) by a dollar while keeping your bundle fixed and your bill rises by \(h_i\), the number of units you buy. So

\[\frac{\partial e}{\partial p_i} = h_i(p,\bar u).\]

The practical reading: the slope of the cost-of-living function with respect to a price is your consumption of that good. Applied to a firm’s cost function it gives \(\partial c/\partial w_j = x_j\): a $1 wage rise increases costs by the number of hours currently employed.

Hotelling’s Lemma is the same calculation for a firm maximising profit \(\pi(p,w) = \max_x \{p\,F(x) - w\cdot x\}\). If the output price rises by a dollar and the firm changes nothing, profit rises by one dollar per unit produced:

\[\frac{\partial \pi}{\partial p} = y^*(p,w), \qquad \frac{\partial \pi}{\partial w_j} = -x_j^*(p,w).\]

The firm will also expand output in response, but by the flat-hilltop argument that adjustment adds nothing at the margin.

Roy’s Identity looks the most mysterious, but it is the same idea plus a conversion of units. The indirect utility function \(v(p,m)\) gives the best utility reachable with prices \(p\) and income \(m\). A rise in \(p_i\), holding the bundle fixed, takes away \(x_i\) dollars of purchasing power, exactly like an income loss of \(x_i\). So

\[\frac{\partial v}{\partial p_i} = -\lambda\, x_i^*, \qquad \frac{\partial v}{\partial m} = \lambda,\]

where \(\lambda\) is the utility value of one extra dollar. Dividing the two converts utility back into dollars, and \(\lambda\) cancels:

\[x_i^*(p,m) = -\frac{\partial v/\partial p_i}{\partial v/\partial m}.\]

The useful part: if you know how well-being depends on prices and income, you can read off demand without solving the consumer’s problem again.

The Le Chatelier principle asks how a firm’s demand for an input responds to its price in the short run, when another input \(x_2\) is fixed, compared with the long run, when it can adjust. Both cost functions satisfy Shephard’s Lemma, so both slopes equal the quantity demanded at the reference price, and the two cost curves touch there. Away from that point, the long-run firm can do everything the short-run firm can and more, so its costs are weakly lower: the long-run cost curve is the envelope of the short-run ones. Two curves that touch, with one always below the other, must have the lower one bending more sharply. Translating curvature into demand slopes gives

\[\left|\frac{\partial x_1^{\mathrm{LR}}}{\partial w_1}\right| \ \ge\ \left|\frac{\partial x_1^{\mathrm{SR}}}{\partial w_1}\right|.\]

In plain terms: more flexibility means a bigger response to prices. This is why demand for energy, or for labour, is usually estimated to be more elastic over several years than over a few months.

Why they are the same. In all four cases the answer comes from the direct effect alone, because the agent is already at an optimum and small re-adjustments are worth nothing to first order. The lemmas differ only in which problem they apply to and which price is changing.

Why “envelope”?

The name comes from a picture. Fix any bundle and ask how much it costs as \(p_1\) changes. The answer is a straight line, because you are buying fixed quantities. Every bundle gives a different line. Someone who re-optimises at every price always picks the cheapest of these lines, so their spending traces out the lower envelope of the whole family.

Show code 
library(ggplot2)

h1 <- function(p1) a * e(p1) / p1
p_grid <- seq(0.5, 4, length.out = 200)

fixed_bundle_line <- function(p_anchor) {
  x1 <- h1(p_anchor)
  x2 <- (1 - a) * e(p_anchor) / p2
  data.frame(p1 = p_grid, cost = p_grid * x1 + p2 * x2, anchor = p_anchor)
}

lines_df <- do.call(rbind, lapply(c(0.75, 1.25, 2, 3), fixed_bundle_line))
env_df   <- data.frame(p1 = p_grid, cost = e(p_grid))
touch_df <- data.frame(p1 = c(0.75, 1.25, 2, 3), cost = e(c(0.75, 1.25, 2, 3)))

ggplot() +
  geom_line(data = lines_df, aes(p1, cost, group = anchor),
            colour = "grey65", linewidth = 0.5) +
  geom_line(data = env_df, aes(p1, cost), linewidth = 1) +
  geom_point(data = touch_df, aes(p1, cost), size = 2) +
  coord_cartesian(ylim = c(0, 6)) +
  labs(x = expression("Price of good 1, " * p[1]),
       y = "Spending needed to reach utility 1") +
  theme_minimal(base_size = 12)

Three features of the chart are the theory in visual form:

  • The lines touch the curve rather than cross it. At each dot the fixed bundle is the optimal one, so the line and the curve have the same slope. The slope of a grey line is the quantity of good 1 in that bundle, so the slope of the expenditure function equals current consumption. That is Shephard’s Lemma.
  • The grey lines lie above the curve everywhere else. Keeping a fixed bundle can never be cheaper than re-optimising. This is why the shortcut in the table always overstated the cost, and why the CPI’s fixed basket overstates inflation.
  • The gap opens slowly near each dot. Close to the touching point, the line and the curve are almost indistinguishable, and the gap widens with the square of the distance. That is the “error grows with the square” pattern in the table.

Data. No external data. All numbers and curves are generated from the closed-form Cobb-Douglas expenditure function in the code above, with \(a = 0.5\), \(\bar u = 1\) and \(p_2 = 1\).

Takeaway

When a price or other parameter changes by a small amount, the effect on an optimised outcome is just the direct effect on what you are currently doing: current quantity times the price change. You do not need to know how people or firms will adjust, because at an optimum small adjustments are worth nothing to first order. That one idea is Shephard’s Lemma, Hotelling’s Lemma, Roy’s Identity and the Le Chatelier principle.

The practical rule and its limit go together. For small changes, such as a modest tax or a 5% price rise, current quantities give a reliable estimate. For large changes the shortcut overstates the cost, because people do substitute, and the error grows quickly. In those cases you need to model behaviour, and the difference between the two answers is a measure of how much substitution is worth.
