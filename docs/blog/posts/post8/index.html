Every month the Bureau of Labor Statistics publishes an unemployment rate, and every month the news reports how it moved. The number is treated as a fact about the economy. It is not: it is an estimate from a sample of roughly 100,000 people, and a different sample of the same size would have produced a slightly different number. How much does that matter? This post answers it by simulation: how precise the rate is, whether the interval we publish really contains the truth, and what a smaller survey would have concluded.

The data are the IPUMS Current Population Survey basic monthly microdata, January 2019 to August 2026. I compare January 2019 with January 2024 — the same month in different years, so the labour market’s seasonal pattern cancels out — and ask whether the unemployment rate really changed. It was 4.47% in January 2019 (97,181 people) and 4.12% in January 2024 (80,923 people): a fall of 0.35 percentage points. Is that a change in the economy, or a change in who answered the phone?

1. Build the interval by resampling

The usual way to put an interval around an estimate is a formula that assumes the sample was drawn at random from a population of equal-weight people. The CPS is neither simple nor equal-weighted — its final person weights run from 177 to 13,140 — so we resample instead: draw the same number of interviewed people at random with replacement, recompute the weighted rate, and repeat 2,000 times. The spread of those 2,000 numbers is the bootstrap distribution: how much the estimate would have moved if we had interviewed somebody else.

The bootstrap interval for January 2024 is 3.92% to 4.32%, and for the change between the two months -0.64 to -0.08 percentage points. The textbook interval for the same change is -0.54 to -0.16 — 49% narrower. That gap is a design effect: the textbook formula understates the real sampling error of the CPS by almost half, so every interval built that way is too narrow.

2. Does a 95% interval contain the truth 95% of the time?

An interval is a promise about how often it will contain the truth, and promises should be checked: build a population with a known unemployment rate, sample it over and over, and count how often the interval contains the truth.

The promise is kept: at 95%, coverage is 93.2% with 250 interviews and 95.0% with 80,923, and every other nominal level lands within about a point of what it promised.

The same test on the real CPS data is less comfortable. Repeating the experiment 1,000 times with smaller surveys, the bootstrap interval contains the full-sample estimate about 92% of the time; the textbook interval about 80% of the time. The bootstrap is at least honest about a weighted proportion being a lopsided statistic. The formula is not.

3. A p-value is an area under a simulated null distribution

To say “the rate changed”, we test H0: the two months have the same rate. Under H0 the estimated change is still random, with a distribution centred on zero. Simulating that experiment 20,000 times gives the null distribution below; everything outside ±0.29 points is the rejection region, 5% of the null distribution by construction.

The observed change of -0.35 points falls just inside that region, and the p-value is simply the area of the null distribution beyond what we observed: 0.016. The textbook formula, using the too-small standard error, would have said 0.014. Both are correct arithmetic; only one is honest about how much the estimate can move.

4. How much could this survey have found?

The most useful thing a simulation tells you is what a survey could have found. The power to detect the change we measured rises from 5.8% with 1,000 interviews per month to 66.8% with 80,923.

The interactive figure below puts that in your hands: drag the slider to change how many people were interviewed each month, and watch the statistic move, the p-value fall, and the verdict flip. It assumes the two months are the same size, so at the top of the slider it reads a p-value of 0.019, slightly above the 0.016 that the two real months (97,181 and 80,923 people) gave.

A survey of 1,000 people a month would have missed this real change 94% of the time and reported “no change”. Even the full CPS sample has roughly a one-in-three chance of missing a change this size. A p-value of 0.016 is therefore not a statement that the change was economically important — it is a statement that this sample could see it.

Takeaway

A confidence interval and a p-value are statements about the data you happened to collect, not about the world. The bootstrap says how much your estimate would move if you collected slightly different data; simulation says how often the interval you built would have been right; and the null distribution turns “significant” into a tail area you can point at. None of this replaces judgement, but it puts a number on the part of a conclusion that came from luck.

The unemployment rate fell by 0.35 points between January 2019 and January 2024, and this sample is large enough to notice it: the 95% interval runs from -0.64 to -0.08 points, and the simulated p-value is 0.016. The same comparison in a survey a tenth the size would have been indistinguishable from noise — and a confident headline would have said nothing changed. Simulation is what tells the two apart.
